Will AI make human work worthless — or priceless?

It depends on two numbers we're only beginning to measure — and here's the strange part: the answer flips depending on when you ask.

If you're just starting out, you can probably feel it already — the first rung of the ladder is harder to reach than it used to be. Here's the number behind that feeling.

The price of machine thinking just collapsed. The cost of running a frontier AI model has fallen roughly 750× in six years — from tens of dollars per million words to fractions of a cent.

So what happens to the value of human thinking? You'll get two opposite answers — and they're both right, at different times.

Two stories everyone tells about AI and work

Illustrative. Each side caricatures a real strand of the academic debate; both cite real economics.

Two stories. Both loud. Both citing real economics.

The Doom story. AI gets cheap. Machines replace cognitive workers. Wages for office jobs collapse the way factory wages did in the 1980s. The new factory worker is the middle manager.

The Utopia story. AI gets cheap. Productivity explodes. Human work that can't be automated — teaching, therapy, judgment, presence — becomes precious. Everyone gets richer, slowly.

Both camps are loud. Both cite real economics. This piece argues they're not actually in conflict — they describe different time periods of the same process. The squeeze is real. So is the rebound. They just don't happen at the same time.

Michael I. Jordan, the Berkeley computer scientist, puts it this way: a market that delivers food into New York every day is itself a kind of intelligence. AI's mistake is trying to rebuild that kind of intelligence inside one model — instead of designing the incentives between many. — paraphrased from Jordan (2025), “A Collectivist, Economic Perspective on AI”; quoted material from Jordan's ICASSP 2023 keynote

Before we resolve the doom-vs-utopia debate, look at where the wave actually lands. Some jobs are mostly routine thinking — the kind a careful intern with a textbook could do. Those are the jobs AI copies most easily.

Data entry, paralegal research, and routine financial analysis sit at the top of that list.

Hands-on, in-person, relationship-based work — nurses, therapists, teachers, tradespeople — barely moves.

Keep this split in mind. The work AI can't copy is about to become the whole story.

First, the squeeze.

Here is the half the Doom camp gets right. When a machine takes over part of a job, the workers doing that part get pushed out — and pay for that kind of work falls. Economists call this the displacement effect.

High-skill wages — the solid black line at top — have climbed steadily since 1980.

Low-skill, in-person service wages — the dashed gray line — have roughly held.

Middle-skill wages — the red line at the bottom — have been hollowed out for forty years. Routine clerical and factory work was the first wave to be automated. White-collar routine work — entry-level analysis, paralegal work, basic coding — is the second.

Honest caveat

This pain is real, and it comes first. Anyone promising a painless transition is selling something. The Doom camp's mistake isn't seeing the squeeze — it's stopping the story there.

Then, the flip.

Here is the half the Utopia camp gets right. Once cheap machine capacity is everywhere, the bottleneck stops being the machine and starts being the work only humans can do. Scarce things get more expensive. We have watched this happen before — twice.

England, 1800. The power loom made cloth roughly ten times cheaper. Handloom weavers — a skilled trade — saw their real wages collapse by about three-quarters in a single generation, and they never recovered. No institution arose in time to protect the value of hand-weaving.

France, 1839. The daguerreotype made a portrait nearly free, and the commercial-portrait trade cratered the same way. But painting recovered — because the art world built something the weavers never got: a market for human-origin work. “Made by a human” became the product. Impressionism was born on the far side of that shock.

Same kind of shock. Same displacement. Opposite endings — and the difference was institutional, not technological. (It is the same reason teaching and nursing, work that can't be sped up, keep getting more expensive: economists call it Baumol cost disease.)

Scarce human work doesn't automatically win. Whether it becomes priceless depends on whether someone builds the market for it — and builds it in time. History points to the lever; it doesn't promise the outcome.

So which story wins? It comes down to two dials.

Whether that long-run rebound actually happens isn't guaranteed. It depends on two things economists can measure in principle — but mostly haven't yet.

Dial A — the consumer side. When you buy a poem, a piece of code, a song, a medical opinion — do you care if a human made it? If buyers treat human-made and AI-made as fully interchangeable, the cheap AI version wins everywhere. If buyers increasingly prefer human-made, human work gains value.

Dial B — the production side. When a machine arrives in a workplace, does it replace the worker doing a task, or does it make that worker more productive? If it replaces, wages fall. If it complements, wages rise.

Drag either dial toward its human-favoring end. In this model, either one alone is enough to flip the long-run outcome from falling to rising. The only configuration where the pessimists win is both dials sitting at the unfavorable end.

What the evidence currently says

What little evidence we have hints that machines often complement skilled workers in real workplaces — Dial B leans good. Whether buyers will keep paying for “human-made” once AI output is indistinguishable is genuinely unknown — Dial A is the open question.

The optimistic case is plausible. But it hangs on a number nobody has pinned down.

A complete view of AI needs three lenses, not one

After Jordan (2019, 2025): the computational, statistical, and economic foundations of AI systems.

Cheap, powerful AI still doesn't guarantee good outcomes.

Even a great tool makes things worse if the rules around it reward bad behavior.

Jordan's argument: real AI systems sit at the intersection of three traditions. Miss any one and you misread the system.

Computing. The algorithms and the raw scale that make modern AI possible. The part that gets all the attention.

Inference. The statistics. The part that tells you when the model is uncertain — or confidently wrong.

Economics. The incentives. What people will actually do with the tool, once it is released into a world full of self-interested players.

Release a powerful AI into a world of people acting in their own interest, and the system settles at whatever those incentives reward — not at whatever's best. Whoever designs the rules decides who actually gains. — paraphrased from Jordan

The two dials in the previous act look like fixed, unknowable parameters — things we just haven't measured yet. Jordan's framework reveals something more unsettling: they are being actively moved right now, by actors with financial incentives to move them in one direction.

Platforms have strong reasons to make AI output indistinguishable from human output — eroding the consumer preference that makes Dial A favorable. Firms face cost pressures that favor replacing workers over complementing them — pushing Dial B the wrong way. The dials are not passively unknown. They are contested.

Machine thinking is becoming as cheap as electricity.

That won't make human minds worthless — but whether it makes them priceless depends on two dials we're only beginning to measure, and on who gets to set them.

The future isn't a forecast you obey. It's an equilibrium you design — and some actors have already started choosing.

How this was made

This piece argues a conditional claim: as AI commoditizes generic thinking, the human work that cannot be automated becomes relatively scarcer — but only under specific conditions on two substitution elasticities. The visualizations illustrate that argument; they do not constitute a forecast. The argument does not rest on any single number; it rests on the equilibrium logic.

Put plainly: the same model that says when AI will collapse human wages also says when it will not — and the difference is decided by institutional choices being made right now, not by the technology itself.

About the centerpieces

  • The sign-flip timeline (Act 4) renders the short-run-vs-long-run sign reversal of the partial derivative of human wages with respect to AI productivity. With capital fixed, displacement dominates and the derivative is negative; once capital adjusts and the work only humans can do becomes the binding constraint, the derivative is positive. The transition is governed by the time scale of capital adjustment in general-purpose-technology rollouts (roughly 5–15 years; Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson 2021).
  • The historical natural experiment (Act 4) contrasts two ~10× cost-collapse shocks a generation apart. The handloom-weaver wage collapse (c. 1800–1860) is documented: real weekly wages fell by roughly three-quarters as the power loom diffused (Bythell 1969; Hobsbawm). The painter trajectory — a sharp mid-century dip as photography destroyed the commercial-portrait trade, then recovery into a fine-art/authenticity premium (Impressionism, from 1874) — is the author's synthesis of the qualitative record (McCauley 1994; Galenson), not a measured wage index. Each series is indexed to 100 at its own start, so the shapes are comparable but the levels are not a cross-series wage comparison. The argument is institutional: same shock, different institutional response, opposite ending.
  • The two-dial widget (Act 5) is a stylized teaching model, not a calibrated forecast. It is derived from a formal nested-CES general-equilibrium model whose central result is that the long-run human wage premium rises only when at least one of two substitution elasticities sits below unity — on the consumer side (Dial A) or on the production side (Dial B). The widget's slider mapping is monotone and directionally faithful to that disjunction; it is not fit to data. The arrow it shows is the direction each dial pushes, not the magnitude.
  • The contestation beat (Act 6) pairs two real-world figures — one per dial — with directional arrows pointing outward, away from the human-favoring centerline. The 78% Dial-A figure comes from a peer-reviewed cross-national study fielded in 2022; because image-generation models have improved since, that number is a conservative floor on present-day misclassification rates, not a current estimate. The 41% Dial-B figure is employers' stated intent to reduce headcount via AI by 2030, not realized layoffs. The companion WEF report also projects +78M net new jobs across the same period — the 41% is one half of a tension the piece flags but does not resolve.

What's verified, what's illustrative

  • Verified pattern, rounded values. The cost of frontier AI inference has fallen by roughly two-to-three orders of magnitude over 2020–2026 (Act 0). The exact yearly figures shown are smoothed for narrative clarity; the magnitude of the drop is real and well-documented across multiple providers.
  • Verified pattern, illustrative magnitudes. The wage polarization in Act 3 — rising high-skill wages, flat low-skill wages, hollowed-out middle — is one of the most-replicated findings in U.S. labor economics (Autor & Dorn 2013; later BLS work). The index values shown are indicative.
  • Documented collapse, illustrative counterpart (Act 4 historical chart). The handloom-weaver wage collapse is a documented historical fact (Bythell 1969; Hobsbawm); the smoothed decade index illustrates that well-established ~75% decline. The painter recovery curve beside it is illustrative — the author's synthesis of the qualitative record (McCauley 1994; Galenson), not a measured series. Each line is indexed to 100 at its own start.
  • Verified figures, properly cited. The two consumer-preference statistics in Act 4 (42% pay-for-human; 17% handmade premium) are real, published, peer-reviewed-or-nationally-representative figures — see Data Sources below.
  • Verified figures, with important caveats. The two contestation figures in Act 6 — 78% AI-image misclassification (Dial A) and 41% employer intent to reduce headcount (Dial B) — are real, cited figures. The 78% comes from a study fielded in 2022; given improvements in image-generation since, it is a conservative floor on present-day rates, not a current estimate. The 41% reflects stated intent, not realized job losses, and sits alongside a WEF projection of +78M net new jobs over the same period — a tension the piece flags but does not resolve.
  • Illustrative throughout. The per-occupation exposure scores (Act 2), the doom/utopia trajectories (Act 1), the sign-flip curve (Act 4), the painter recovery curve (Act 4), and the two-dial trajectory (Act 5) are author's synthesis. They communicate qualitative ordering and direction, not point estimates.
